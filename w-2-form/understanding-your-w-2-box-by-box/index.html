Understanding Your W-2: A Box-by-Box Guide

What every box on Form W-2 means, why Box 1 rarely matches your salary, how to reconcile the form against your final pay stub, and what to do if it is wrong.

By Alexander Caldwell · August 13, 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • Verify your Social Security number before anything else; a wrong digit misroutes your earnings record.
  • Box 1 is federal taxable wages and is normally lower than your salary because of pre-tax deductions.
  • Boxes 1, 3 and 5 differ by design: 401(k) contributions and the Social Security wage cap treat them differently.
  • Box 12 codes report items such as D for 401(k), W for HSA and DD for the cost of health coverage.
  • Reconcile the form against your final pay stub's year-to-date columns before you file.

Form W-2, the Wage and Tax Statement, is the one payroll document nearly everyone touches. Employers must furnish it by January 31, and a copy goes to the Social Security Administration, which shares it with the IRS. Because the IRS matches your return against that copy, a W-2 you do not understand is a W-2 you cannot check.

The identification boxes

Boxes a through f carry your Social Security number, the employer's EIN, and both addresses. Verify your SSN first. A single wrong digit means your earnings are not credited to your Social Security record, and fixing it later is far harder than fixing it in February.

The money boxes

BoxLabelWhat it contains
1Wages, tips, other compensationTaxable wages for federal income tax: gross pay minus pre-tax deductions such as 401(k), health premiums and FSA, plus taxable benefits.
2Federal income tax withheldThe total federal withholding from all your paychecks. This is the number that becomes a credit on your tax return.
3Social Security wagesWages subject to Social Security tax, capped at the annual wage base. Usually higher than Box 1 because 401(k) contributions are included.
4Social Security tax withheld6.2% of Box 3.
5Medicare wages and tipsLike Box 3 but with no cap.
6Medicare tax withheld1.45% of Box 5, plus 0.9% on wages over $200,000.
7 and 8Social Security tips / allocated tipsRelevant only to tipped employees.
10Dependent care benefitsAmounts run through a dependent care FSA.
11Nonqualified plansDistributions from certain deferred compensation plans.

Why Box 1, Box 3 and Box 5 are different

This is the question that fills my inbox every February. Each box measures wages under a different set of rules:

  • A traditional 401(k) contribution reduces Box 1 but not Boxes 3 and 5.
  • Health premiums, HSA and FSA contributions under a Section 125 plan reduce all three.
  • The Social Security wage base caps Box 3 only. High earners will see Box 5 exceed Box 3.
  • Taxable fringe benefits, such as group life insurance over $50,000 or personal use of a company car, are added to all three.

So Box 1 being lower than your salary is normal. Box 1 being lower than your salary by an amount you cannot explain is not.

Box 12: the code list

Box 12 uses letter codes to report items that need their own line. The ones you are most likely to see:

  • D: traditional 401(k) contributions. AA: Roth 401(k). E and BB: the 403(b) equivalents.
  • W: employer and employee HSA contributions made through payroll.
  • DD: total cost of employer-sponsored health coverage. Informational only; it is not taxable.
  • C: taxable cost of group life insurance over $50,000.

Box 13 and Box 14

Box 13 has three checkboxes. “Retirement plan” matters because it affects whether your IRA contributions are deductible. Box 14 is a catch-all where employers report things like state disability insurance, union dues, or after-tax retirement contributions. The labels are set by the employer, so ask payroll if one is unclear.

Boxes 15 through 20: state and local

These repeat the wage and withholding figures for each state and locality that taxed you. If you worked in two states, you should see two rows, and the state wages may not add up to Box 1 because states define taxable wages differently.

Reconcile against your final pay stub

Take your last pay stub of the year and compare the year-to-date columns to the W-2. The YTD federal withholding should equal Box 2 exactly. YTD Social Security tax should equal Box 4. YTD gross pay minus YTD pre-tax deductions (excluding 401(k)) should equal Boxes 3 and 5 unless the wage base applied. Any difference is a question for payroll before you file.

If the W-2 is wrong or missing

Contact your employer first; they can issue a corrected Form W-2c. If it does not arrive by mid-February, you can call the IRS, which will contact the employer on your behalf. As a last resort you can file using Form 4852 with figures from your final pay stub, which is one more reason to keep that stub safe.

Keep it: the IRS recommends keeping W-2s with your tax records for at least three years, but I suggest keeping them indefinitely. They are your proof of Social Security earnings, and the SSA's record is not always complete.

What Does a Pay Stub Look Like and What Information Does It Include?

A pay stub is more than the final deposit figure. Here is how a typical statement is structured, what each section reports, and which fields are worth checking every pay period.

By Alexander Caldwell · September 3, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • A pay stub is organised into identifying details, earnings, deductions and a summary of gross and net pay.
  • The earnings section explains how gross pay was built: hourly rates, overtime, salary, bonuses and commissions.
  • Gross pay minus taxes and other deductions equals net pay, and a large gap between the two is not automatically an error.
  • Year-to-date columns show what has accumulated since January and give context a single paycheck cannot.
  • A quick check of pay period, hours, rate, deductions, net pay and YTD totals catches most obvious discrepancies.

A pay stub gives you a detailed breakdown of your earnings for a specific pay period. Although the exact design can differ between employers and payroll systems, most pay stubs follow a similar structure. They identify the employer and employee, show the pay period, break down earnings and deductions and explain how gross pay becomes net pay.

For employees, understanding these sections makes it easier to review each paycheck rather than looking only at the final amount deposited into a bank account. For employers and payroll teams, the same information provides an organized record of how an employee's pay was calculated.

What Does a Typical Pay Stub Look Like?

A typical pay stub is divided into several sections rather than presenting one final payment figure.

At the top, you will generally find identifying and payroll information. This may include the employer's name, employee name, pay date and the beginning and ending dates of the pay period. Depending on the payroll system, it may also identify the employee's department, employee number or pay frequency.

The main body of the pay stub usually contains earnings, taxes and other deductions. Current pay period amounts are commonly displayed alongside year-to-date figures.

Toward the bottom, or in a separate summary area, you will normally find gross pay, total deductions and net pay.

The design can change considerably from one employer to another, but these basic pieces of information are what make a pay stub understandable.

Understanding the Earnings Section

The earnings section explains how much an employee earned before taxes and other deductions were removed.

For an hourly employee, this section can include the hourly rate, number of regular hours worked and regular earnings. If overtime applies, the overtime hours, rate and earnings may appear separately.

A salaried employee's pay stub can look different because compensation is generally based on an annual salary divided across the employer's payroll schedule rather than individual hours worked.

Additional forms of compensation may also appear here when applicable. Bonuses, commissions or other earnings can be listed separately instead of being combined into one unexplained number.

This is why checking the earnings section is useful. The gross pay figure tells you how much was earned before deductions, while the individual entries explain where that total came from.

Gross Pay and Net Pay Are Not the Same Thing

One of the most important distinctions on a pay stub is the difference between gross pay and net pay.

Gross pay is the employee's earnings before applicable taxes and deductions are removed. Net pay is what remains after those amounts have been deducted.

At its simplest, the relationship looks like this:

Gross Pay − Taxes − Other Deductions = Net Pay

The difference between these two figures can sometimes be significant. That does not necessarily indicate a payroll problem. Taxes, insurance premiums, retirement contributions and other deductions can all reduce the amount ultimately received by an employee.

Rather than comparing only gross and net pay, I recommend looking at the individual deductions between them.

Taxes and Other Deductions

The deductions section is often the part of a pay stub that requires the most attention because several different items may appear there.

Depending on the employee's circumstances and location, payroll tax entries can include federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare and applicable state or local taxes.

Other deductions may relate to benefits or programs selected by the employee. Health insurance premiums and retirement contributions are common examples, although the exact deductions vary considerably between workplaces.

A deduction appearing on one person's pay stub does not mean that it should appear on another person's statement. Payroll requirements and withholding can depend on factors such as worker classification, location, compensation and individual elections.

When reviewing a pay stub, it is therefore more useful to understand what each deduction represents than to compare the statement directly with someone else's.

Why Year-to-Date Numbers Matter

The year-to-date section is one of the most useful parts of a pay stub, yet it is easy to overlook.

Current figures tell you what happened during one pay period. Year-to-date figures show how earnings, taxes and deductions have accumulated since the beginning of the year.

Suppose an employee wants to know how much federal income tax has been withheld so far. Looking at the current deduction only answers that question for one paycheck. The YTD column provides the cumulative amount recorded through the current pay period.

The same principle applies to gross earnings and many other deductions.

I find YTD figures particularly useful when reviewing payroll records because they provide context that a single paycheck cannot.

What Should You Check on Your Pay Stub?

You do not need to perform a complete payroll audit every time you receive a paycheck. A quick review of several important fields can still help you identify obvious discrepancies.

Start with the pay period and pay date. Then check that your regular hours, overtime and pay rate are represented correctly when those fields apply to your compensation.

Next, compare your gross earnings with the individual earnings entries. Review the listed taxes and deductions before checking the resulting net pay.

Finally, look at the YTD amounts. If something changes unexpectedly from one pay period to another, the individual entries can often help you identify where that change occurred.

If you find something you do not understand, asking your employer or payroll department for clarification is generally better than assuming the figure is incorrect.

Why Keeping Pay Stubs Can Be Useful

Pay stubs provide a running record of compensation, withholding and deductions.

Keeping copies can make it easier to review past earnings, compare payroll periods and maintain personal financial records. They can also be useful when you need to understand how a particular payment was calculated months after receiving it.

Digital payroll systems have made these records easier to access, but I still recommend knowing where your historical statements are stored and periodically checking that you can retrieve them.

A pay stub should ultimately make your compensation easier to understand, not simply document the final amount you were paid.

A Closer Look at a Real Pay Stub Structure

Once you know what each section represents, reading a pay stub becomes much easier. You can move from the pay period to earnings, follow the deductions and then see exactly how the statement arrives at net pay.

There are still details that can vary depending on the employee, employer and location. Seeing those fields together in an example is often more useful than looking at them individually.

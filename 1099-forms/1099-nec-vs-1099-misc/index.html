1099-NEC vs. 1099-MISC: Which One Applies to You?

How the two most common 1099 forms differ, who must issue them, what freelancers should do when one arrives, and how 1099 income is taxed compared with a W-2 paycheck.

By Alexander Caldwell · August 10, 2026 · 3 min read

If you have done any freelance or contract work, a Form 1099 will show up in late January. There are more than a dozen types, but two account for most of the confusion: Form 1099-NEC and Form 1099-MISC. Since the IRS split them in 2020, business owners regularly ask me which to file, and contractors ask why they received one and not the other.

Form 1099-NEC: nonemployee compensation

The NEC form reports payments of $600 or more made during the year to someone who is not an employee, for services performed in the course of your trade or business. Freelance designers, consultants, contract developers, gig drivers paid directly by a business, and independent sales agents all land here. The payer must send Copy B to the contractor and file with the IRS by January 31, with no automatic extension.

The single important box is Box 1, nonemployee compensation. If federal tax was withheld (rare, and usually because of backup withholding for a missing taxpayer identification number), it appears in Box 4.

Form 1099-MISC: everything else

Before 2020, contractor pay lived in Box 7 of the MISC form. Today the MISC form is reserved for other kinds of payments, including:

  • Box 1: rents of $600 or more, such as office space paid to an individual landlord.
  • Box 2: royalties of $10 or more.
  • Box 3: other income, including prizes, awards and certain settlement payments.
  • Box 6: medical and health care payments.
  • Box 10: gross proceeds paid to an attorney.

The MISC form is due to recipients by January 31 but, unlike the NEC, is due to the IRS at the end of February on paper or the end of March electronically.

Quick decision guide for business owners

You paidForm
A freelancer or contractor for services1099-NEC
An individual for rent1099-MISC, Box 1
A prize or award winner1099-MISC, Box 3
A corporation for services (other than legal or medical)Usually no 1099 required
Anyone by credit card or a payment appNo 1099 from you; the processor files Form 1099-K
An employeeForm W-2, never a 1099

Collect a Form W-9 from every vendor before the first payment. It gives you the legal name, TIN and entity type you need to decide whether a 1099 is required and to file it without errors.

What to do when you receive one

First, check the amount against your own records. Payers make mistakes, and the IRS matches the form against your return. If the figure is wrong, ask for a corrected form; do not simply report a different number. Second, remember that you owe tax on the income whether or not a form arrives. The $600 threshold is a reporting rule for the payer, not an exemption for you.

How 1099 income is taxed differently from a paycheck

On a W-2, your employer withholds income tax and pays half of your Social Security and Medicare. On a 1099, nobody withholds anything, and you pay both halves of those payroll taxes as self-employment tax, currently 15.3% on net earnings (with half deductible). Three habits keep this manageable:

  1. Set aside 25 to 30 percent of every payment in a separate account for taxes.
  2. Make quarterly estimated payments (April, June, September and January) to avoid underpayment penalties.
  3. Track deductible expenses, because self-employment tax is calculated on net profit, not gross receipts.

Income from a 1099-NEC is reported on Schedule C; the profit flows to Schedule SE for self-employment tax and to Form 1040. Rent from a 1099-MISC usually goes on Schedule E, and prizes in Box 3 are reported as other income.

Got a 1099 but you were really an employee? If the business controlled your hours, tools and methods, you may have been misclassified. Form SS-8 asks the IRS to determine your status, and Form 8919 lets you pay only the employee share of Social Security and Medicare while the question is resolved.

Florida is one of a small group of states that does not tax personal income. For employees this means a simpler pay stub: there is no state income tax line, because there is no state income tax to withhold.

What Still Comes Out

The absence of state income tax does not change federal obligations. A Florida paycheck still shows federal income tax withheld according to your Form W-4, Social Security tax at 6.2% up to the annual wage base, and Medicare tax at 1.45% on all covered wages with an additional amount above the statutory threshold. Benefit deductions, retirement contributions and garnishments all behave as they would anywhere.

So the practical difference between a Florida paycheck and one from a state with a 5% income tax is roughly 5% of taxable wages, which is substantial but narrower than people often assume.

What Employers Pay

Florida employers pay reemployment tax, the state's unemployment insurance contribution, on wages up to a state wage base. This is an employer tax and is not deducted from employee pay. If you see a deduction on a Florida stub that you think might be unemployment tax, query it with payroll.

Where the Revenue Comes From

With no personal income tax, Florida leans heavily on sales tax, documentary stamp taxes and tourism-related revenue. That mix is why the state's finances are more sensitive to consumer spending cycles than those of income-tax states, and it is worth factoring in if you are weighing a move purely on tax grounds.

A Note for Remote Workers

Living in Florida while working for an employer in another state does not automatically remove every state tax question. Where services are performed matters, and some states have their own sourcing rules for remote work. If you moved to Florida mid-year or travel back to work in another state, check how that state treats the wages before assuming a zero state liability.

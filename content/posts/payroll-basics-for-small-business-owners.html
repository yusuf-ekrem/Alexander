title: Payroll Basics for Small Business Owners: A Step-by-Step Guide slug: payroll-basics-for-small-business-owners category: payroll date: 2026-08-27 description: From your first hire to your first quarterly filing, here is the payroll process explained in order, with the deadlines and documents that trip up new employers. ---

Hiring your first employee is exciting right up until someone mentions payroll taxes. The rules are not actually complicated, but they are unforgiving: the IRS penalises late deposits on a sliding scale, and states are no more lenient. This guide lays out the process in the order you will actually encounter it.

Step 1: Register as an employer

Before you can pay anyone, you need an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS. It is free and takes minutes online. Then register with your state for withholding tax and unemployment insurance (SUI). Some states and cities have additional accounts for paid family leave or local income tax. Registering late is the most common first mistake I see, because the state will still assess unemployment tax from the first day of wages.

Step 2: Collect the right paperwork from each employee

  • Form W-4: tells you how much federal income tax to withhold. Many states have their own equivalent.
  • Form I-9: verifies work eligibility. It must be completed within three business days of the start date and kept on file, not sent to the government.
  • Direct deposit authorisation, and any benefit enrolment forms.
  • New hire report: most states require you to report new employees within 20 days.

Step 3: Classify workers correctly

Deciding whether someone is an employee or an independent contractor is not a matter of preference. The IRS looks at behavioural control, financial control and the nature of the relationship. Misclassifying an employee as a contractor to skip payroll taxes is one of the costliest errors a small business can make, because back taxes, penalties and interest all land at once. When in doubt, treat the worker as an employee.

Step 4: Choose a pay schedule

Weekly, biweekly, semimonthly and monthly are all legal federally, but many states set a minimum frequency for certain workers. Biweekly (26 paydays a year) is the most common choice for hourly staff because overtime aligns cleanly with the workweek. Whatever you choose, publish it and stick to it.

Step 5: Run payroll

Each pay period follows the same sequence:

  1. Collect and approve hours, including overtime and paid leave.
  2. Calculate gross pay for each employee.
  3. Subtract pre-tax deductions such as 401(k) and health premiums.
  4. Calculate and withhold federal income tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), and state and local taxes.
  5. Subtract post-tax deductions such as garnishments or Roth contributions.
  6. Pay the net amount and issue a pay stub. Most states require a stub, on paper or electronically, showing hours, rates, gross pay, deductions and net pay.

Step 6: Deposit the taxes

This is where new employers get hurt. The money you withheld, plus your matching share of Social Security and Medicare, is not yours to hold. Federal deposits are made through EFTPS on either a monthly or semiweekly schedule depending on your lookback liability. New employers usually start as monthly depositors, meaning taxes for August are due by September 15. Federal unemployment tax (FUTA) is deposited quarterly once the liability exceeds $500. State deposit schedules vary, so check yours.

Rule of thumb: move the tax money into a separate account the same day you run payroll. If it is never in your operating account, you will never be tempted to spend it.

Step 8: Keep records

Keep payroll records, timesheets and tax filings for at least four years, and I-9 forms for three years after hire or one year after separation, whichever is later. Good records make audits boring, which is exactly what you want.

Software or a provider?

You can run payroll by hand with the IRS tables, and for one employee some owners do. Beyond that, a payroll service or software pays for itself the first time it prevents a late deposit penalty. Whichever route you take, the owner remains responsible, so review every payroll before it is submitted and reconcile the tax deposits each quarter against Form 941.

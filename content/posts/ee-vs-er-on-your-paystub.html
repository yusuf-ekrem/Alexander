EE and ER are the most useful two-letter codes on a pay stub. Once you know them, a statement that looked like an unexplained wall of deductions resolves into two clear groups: money that left your pay, and money your employer spent on your behalf.

The Rule

EE stands for employee. The amount was withheld from your gross pay and it reduces your net pay. ER stands for employer. The company paid it in addition to your wages and it does not reduce your deposit.

So Medical EE is your share of the health premium and Medical ER is your employer's. Fed MED/EE is your Medicare withholding and Fed MED/ER, where shown, is the employer's matching contribution.

Where the Split Applies

Several payroll items are genuinely shared. Social Security and Medicare are each matched: you pay 6.2% and 1.45%, your employer pays the same. Health insurance is usually split, with the employer carrying the larger share. Retirement plans often involve an employer match alongside your contribution.

Others are one-sided. Federal and state income tax are employee-only. Federal and state unemployment tax are employer-only, which is why you will not find them as deductions on your stub.

Why the ER Column Deserves a Look

Employees read the EE column because it affects the deposit. The ER column is where the rest of your compensation hides. Add up the employer contributions to health coverage, retirement matching and payroll taxes, and the total package is often 20 to 30 percent above the salary figure on your offer letter.

That is worth knowing when comparing jobs, where a higher salary with a weaker benefit contribution can come out behind. I break down how to read both columns and total up real compensation in my guide to EE and ER on a pay stub for Online Paystub.

If Your Stub Shows Only EE

Not every payroll system prints employer contributions, and nothing requires them to. If yours does not, your benefits statement or an annual total compensation summary from HR will usually show the same information in a different place.

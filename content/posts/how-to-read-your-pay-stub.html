A pay stub looks simple until you actually try to check it. Most people glance at the net pay, confirm the deposit landed, and move on. That habit is how small payroll errors quietly turn into hundreds of dollars over a year. After twelve years of reviewing payroll for companies large and small, I can tell you that the stub is the single best tool you have for catching those errors, provided you know what each line means.

The header: who, when, and for which period

The top of the stub identifies the employer, the employee, and two dates that people constantly confuse:

  • Pay period is the span of days you are being paid for, for example August 16 through August 31.
  • Pay date is the day the money is actually paid. It is usually a few days after the period ends.

Always check the pay period first. If you worked overtime in the last week of the month, it may belong to the next stub, not this one.

Earnings: what you were paid for

The earnings section lists every type of pay you received. Hourly employees will see hours multiplied by a rate; salaried employees see a flat amount. Common lines include:

The total of these lines is your gross pay, the number every tax and deduction is calculated from.

Pre-tax deductions

Pre-tax deductions come out before taxes are calculated, which lowers your taxable wages. The usual suspects are traditional 401(k) contributions, health, dental and vision premiums, HSA and FSA contributions, and commuter benefits. If you elected a 6% 401(k) contribution and the stub shows 5%, that is a mistake worth fixing immediately, because you also lose any employer match on the missing amount.

Taxes

This block is where most confusion lives. You will typically see:

  • Federal income tax: withheld based on your Form W-4 and the IRS withholding tables.
  • Social Security: 6.2% of taxable wages up to the annual wage base.
  • Medicare: 1.45% of all taxable wages, plus an additional 0.9% above $200,000.
  • State and local income tax: varies by state; several states have none at all.
  • State disability or paid leave: small percentages in states such as California, New Jersey and New York.

A quick sanity check: Social Security should be almost exactly 6.2% of the taxable wage shown, and Medicare 1.45%. If those percentages are off, the taxable wage figure is probably wrong, and that usually traces back to a pre-tax deduction being coded incorrectly.

Post-tax deductions

These come out after taxes: Roth 401(k) contributions, union dues, wage garnishments, life insurance beyond the tax-free amount, and charitable giving through payroll. They do not reduce your taxable income, so the amounts should match exactly what you agreed to.

Employer contributions

Many stubs include a section that does not affect your net pay at all: the employer's share of your health premium, 401(k) match, and its own payroll taxes. It is informational, but it is useful for understanding your total compensation and for confirming that your match is actually being deposited.

Year-to-date (YTD) columns

Next to almost every line is a YTD figure. These running totals are what your W-2 will show at the end of the year, so they are worth a glance every quarter. If a YTD number jumps by more than the current period amount, a correction or retroactive payment was processed, and you should be able to see it on the stub.

Net pay

Gross pay minus all deductions and taxes equals net pay, the amount deposited. If you split your deposit across accounts, each account and amount should be listed.

Five-minute check every payday: confirm the pay period, verify hours and rate, compare each deduction to your elections, test Social Security at 6.2%, and glance at the YTD totals. Anything that does not add up goes to your payroll or HR contact in writing, with the stub attached.

Keep your stubs

Hold on to at least a year of stubs, and ideally keep the final stub of each year permanently. Lenders ask for them, they resolve disputes over hours, and the December stub is your best evidence if a W-2 ever arrives with the wrong numbers.

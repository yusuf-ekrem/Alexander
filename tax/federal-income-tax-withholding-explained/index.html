Federal Income Tax Withholding Explained: How Your Employer Calculates It

Why the federal tax on your paycheck is an estimate, how Form W-4 shapes it, and how to adjust withholding so you neither owe a surprise bill nor lend the IRS money all year.

By Alexander Caldwell · August 24, 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • Federal withholding is an estimate of your annual tax, not a final bill.
  • Payroll calculates it from your pay, your pay frequency and the entries on your Form W-4.
  • Two-income households usually under-withhold unless Step 2 of the W-4 is completed.
  • Social Security and Medicare are fixed percentages and cannot be adjusted through the W-4.
  • Run the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator once a year and after any major life or income change.

The federal income tax line on your pay stub is not a bill. It is a prepayment, an estimate of what you will owe for the year, spread across your paychecks. When the estimate is close, tax season is uneventful. When it is far off, you either get a large refund (money you overpaid) or a balance due with possible penalties. Understanding how the estimate is built lets you steer it.

Where the number comes from

Your employer does not know your full financial picture. It knows three things: how much it pays you, how often, and what you wrote on Form W-4. Using those inputs and the IRS withholding tables published each year in Publication 15-T, payroll works out the tax for each check. In plain terms, the calculation looks like this:

  1. Take the taxable wages for the pay period (gross pay minus pre-tax deductions).
  2. Annualise them: multiply by the number of pay periods in the year.
  3. Adjust for the W-4 entries: multiple jobs, dependents, other income, and extra deductions.
  4. Apply the standard deduction and the tax brackets to find the annual tax.
  5. Divide by the number of pay periods, and add any extra withholding you requested.

Because every check is treated as if you earn that amount all year, a large bonus or a heavy overtime month is withheld at a higher rate than usual. That is not an error; the tables assume the high income will continue.

The W-4 after 2020

The redesigned Form W-4 dropped the old “allowances” and replaced them with five short steps:

  • Step 1: your filing status. Single, married filing jointly, or head of household.
  • Step 2: multiple jobs or a working spouse. Checking the box, or using the worksheet, prevents under-withholding when two incomes share one standard deduction.
  • Step 3: dependents. Each qualifying child reduces annual withholding by the credit amount.
  • Step 4: other income, deductions beyond the standard deduction, and extra withholding per paycheck.
  • Step 5: signature.

If you only complete Steps 1 and 5, withholding assumes one job, no dependents and the standard deduction, which is accurate for many single filers and wrong for most two-income households.

Withholding on bonuses and commissions

Supplemental wages can be withheld two ways. If they are paid separately from regular wages, employers usually apply a flat rate (22% federally, up to $1 million). If they are combined with a regular paycheck, the whole amount runs through the normal tables. Neither method changes what you ultimately owe; it only changes how much is prepaid.

Social Security and Medicare are different

Federal income tax is the only withholding you can adjust. Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) are flat percentages set by law, not by your W-4. If those lines look wrong, the problem is in the taxable wage figure, not your elections.

How to check whether you are on track

Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator once a year, ideally in late spring after you have a few stubs and last year's return in hand. It compares your projected tax to your year-to-date withholding and tells you exactly what to enter on a new W-4. Run it again whenever life changes:

  • You start or leave a job, or take on a second one.
  • You marry, divorce, or have a child.
  • Your spouse's income changes.
  • You start earning significant side income that has no withholding.

Refund or not? A refund of a few hundred dollars means your estimate was good. A refund of several thousand means you lent the IRS money interest-free all year; a balance due of more than $1,000 can trigger an underpayment penalty. Either way, the fix is a new W-4, which your employer must apply by the start of the first pay period ending 30 days after you submit it.

State withholding

Most states with an income tax use their own withholding form and tables. Some accept the federal W-4; others require a separate certificate. If you move between states mid-year, tell payroll immediately, because withholding for the wrong state creates a filing headache that lasts well into the next year.

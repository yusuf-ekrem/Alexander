Gross Pay vs. Net Pay: Where Does Your Paycheck Go?

A worked example that follows a $4,000 paycheck from gross to net, showing exactly which taxes and deductions take a share and why the numbers differ from one person to the next.

By Alexander Caldwell · August 17, 2026 · 2 min read

“They said $52,000 a year, so why is my check $1,500?” It is one of the most common questions I hear from new hires, and the answer is the difference between gross pay and net pay. Both numbers are correct; they simply measure different things.

Definitions

  • Gross pay is everything you earned in the pay period before anything is taken out: regular wages, overtime, bonuses, commissions and paid leave.
  • Net pay is what remains after taxes and deductions. It is the amount deposited into your account, sometimes called take-home pay.

Everything between the two falls into three groups: pre-tax deductions, taxes, and post-tax deductions. The order matters, because pre-tax deductions shrink the amount that taxes are calculated on.

A worked example

Meet Dana, a single filer in a state with a 5% flat income tax, paid semimonthly (24 checks a year). Dana's gross pay this period is $4,000, and Dana contributes 6% to a traditional 401(k) and pays $150 for health insurance.

StepItemAmountRunning total
1Gross pay$4,000.00
2401(k) contribution (6%, pre-tax)−$240.00$3,760.00
3Health premium (pre-tax)−$150.00$3,610.00 taxable for income tax
4Federal income tax (per W-4 and tables, approx.)−$395.00
5Social Security (6.2% of $3,850*)−$238.70
6Medicare (1.45% of $3,850*)−$55.83
7State income tax (5% of $3,610)−$180.50
8Net pay$2,739.97

*Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce federal and most state income tax, but not Social Security and Medicare, so those are calculated on $4,000 minus the $150 health premium.

Dana's take-home pay is about 68% of gross. Multiply by 24 and the annual picture is $96,000 gross, roughly $65,760 net, with $5,760 saved in the 401(k) along the way.

Why your percentage is different

Two people with identical salaries can have very different net pay. The main levers:

  • Filing status and W-4 entries. A married employee with two children claiming credits on the W-4 will have far less federal tax withheld than a single employee with no adjustments.
  • State and city. Nine states have no wage income tax; a few cities add their own. The same job can net several hundred dollars more per month across a state line.
  • Benefit elections. Family health coverage, dental, vision, FSA and HSA contributions all reduce net pay while (mostly) reducing taxes too.
  • Retirement contributions. A traditional 401(k) lowers taxable wages; a Roth 401(k) does not, so the same percentage produces a smaller net check with a Roth.
  • Garnishments and other post-tax items. Child support, student loan garnishments and union dues come out after tax.

Why the first and last checks of the year look odd

Social Security stops once your wages reach the annual wage base, so high earners see a bump in net pay late in the year. Benefit premiums often change in January, and some employers take premiums from only the first two checks of each month, leaving the third biweekly check unusually large. If a check looks wrong, compare it to the previous one line by line before assuming an error.

Estimating a job offer: as a rough guide, take-home pay lands between 65% and 78% of gross for most employees, lower with family benefits and higher in no-income-tax states. For a precise figure, run the offer through a paycheck calculator with your actual W-4 choices.
